# Evidence extraction contract

## Bringing in a big archive: the three-pass method

**This governs any import too large to read in one sitting** — a notes export, a
photo library, years of mail, a folder of documents. **Read this before you write
a single extraction prompt.** It is not style guidance; each rule below replaced
a specific failure, and the failures are stated so you can recognise them.

### The failure that makes all of this necessary

**A reader who is told who I am will find a more detailed version of what it was
told.** This is not a small effect. In one measured run over a 984-item personal
archive, twelve readers were briefed with the subject's own headline facts — the
handful of things anyone who knew them would list — then asked to flag anything
that contradicted "what the record already believes."

Every finding came back mapped to something already on file. Meanwhile **190
items — 19% of that archive — documented a decade-long home renovation.** It was
mentioned once, and only to say a note was misfiled because it happened to
contain a detail about an already-known injury. **The subject had to name their own
house.** They put it this way:

> everyone is a very three-dimensional character study, not just more extensions
> of the first thing we knew about them

**The output looked excellent from the inside.** Nothing was wrong, nothing
contradicted anything, no error surfaced. The only symptom was an absence — and
absence is the one thing a confident summary hides. **Assume it is happening to
you right now.**

### Pass 0 — write the plan down first

Before touching the material, **publish a plan as a file in the world**, not just
in conversation, so it survives a lost context and I can hold you to it.

| The plan states | |
|---|---|
| **Scope** | how many items, what is excluded and on whose say-so |
| **The passes** | each one's job and what it outputs |
| **Definition of done** | *the records that will exist* — named, not described |
| **Cost** | rough effort per pass |
| **Failure modes** | what is likely to go wrong with this material |

**Why this is step zero.** In the run described above, four passes happened and a
plan was never stated once. Every pass existed because the subject caught
something. The final pass — the one that writes records — existed only because
they asked whether anything had actually been catalogued. It had not.

**Writing down "what does done look like" makes it impossible not to notice that
an index is not a record.** That is the whole value. Then report **bearing** at
every checkpoint: where we are, what changed, what is left, and — say it out
loud — **whether what you are showing me is an intermediate or a deliverable.**

### Pass 1 — Survey, blind

Many readers, fanned out, **each told nothing about me.**

Sort items by size, deal them round-robin so slices are balanced, **60–90 items
per reader.** Use a capable mid-tier model, not your largest: this is faithful
reading and structured reporting, and in the measured run **a blind mid-tier pass
beat a primed frontier pass badly. The frame matters more than the model.**

Give each reader this, close to verbatim:

> You are reading a slice of one person's notes. You will be told NOTHING about
> who they are. That is deliberate — a previous pass was given a biography and
> found only what the biography predicted. Build the picture from the notes alone.
>
> Read EVERY note in your batch, start to finish.
>
> THE QUESTION: **What is this person's life made of?** Not "what dramatic events
> happened to them." What do they spend time on, care about, worry about, build,
> buy, learn, plan, repeat, return to?
>
> RULES THAT OVERRIDE YOUR INSTINCTS:
> 1. **Volume is signal.** Thirty notes on one subject makes it a major dimension
>    even if no single note looks important. Count subjects; report frequencies.
> 2. **Sustained ordinary effort ranks with dramatic events.** A years-long
>    project, a list kept for a decade. No single note announces these — hunt for
>    the same subject recurring years apart.
> 3. **Practices are not events.** Things with no date, no other person and no
>    outcome — a routine, a method, a standard held. Report them prominently.
> 4. **Never rank by drama or word count.** A 40-word note can be the sole
>    evidence of a whole domain.
> 5. **Mundane does not mean shallow.** Domestic, repetitive, administrative
>    material is where a life mostly happens. Do not compress it.
> 6. **People matter more than subject matter.** For everyone named: how often,
>    over what span, in what role, and above all **what this person observed or
>    felt about them.** One line like "he shut down the moment I pushed" outweighs
>    any amount of surrounding subject matter.
> 7. **Report what you cannot classify** — that is the most valuable thing you can
>    produce.
>
> Output: a taxonomy YOU derive (counts, date ranges, ordered by how much of the
> life it occupies); sustained threads of 3+ years; skills and standards; money
> and possessions; places; aspirations and unfinished business; people; dates;
> what surprised you; and a **note-by-note ledger — one line for EVERY item,
> including trivial ones, marked substantive / transactional / empty.**

**Never ask a reader whether something contradicts what is already known.** That
question can only return material adjacent to what you already hold.

**The ledger is not optional.** It is the anti-drop safeguard, and it hands you
the discard pile for free — which is exactly where a biased pass buries its
evidence.

### The arithmetic — mechanical, before any headline

**Do not synthesize from what the readers found interesting.** Each reports its
own top findings against its own slice, so **a subject ranked sixth in every
slice never reaches you — even when the sum makes it the largest thing in the
archive.**

That is not hypothetical. In the measured run, a **13-year habit of scanning and
filing receipts, manuals, warranties and contracts** was ranked below fourth by
**all twelve readers**, who variously called it "unclassifiable,"
"undifferentiated archive matter," "pure fragments," "empty." Summed, it was
**the second-largest thing in that person's world — larger than their entire
career.** No individual reader could have seen it. Only the sum can.

So: **pull raw per-subject counts from every reader, sum them mechanically across
all slices before reading a single headline, re-rank globally, and publish the
table** so the arithmetic is checkable rather than a matter of taste. Merge
synonymous categories **by reading them** — keyword clustering fails on personal
material.

**Medium everywhere is not medium.**

### Pass 2 — Reconcile

**One reader, alone, and now it may know everything.** Priming is forbidden in
pass 1 and required here — that is the whole design. Discovery cannot be done by
someone holding the answer key; reconciliation cannot be done by someone without
it.

The reconciler clusters and sums, **diffs the map against what the world already
holds** — every dimension above threshold with no record is a gap — applies both
rankings below, writes the work plan, and records the limits honestly:
undercounts, reader errors, excluded material.

**Blind reports inherit no authority from having been produced blind.** In the
measured run one reader misdated a collision by a year against a contemporaneous
source. Verify before promoting.

### Pass 3 — Materialize

**This is the pass that writes records, and the one most likely to be skipped**,
because after pass 2 the work *feels* finished. It is not.

**Passes 1 and 2 produce a map. A map is not a catalogue.** In the measured run
the first two passes established that home and property was 32% of the archive and
first in twelve of twelve slices — and afterwards the world still had no record of
a house at all.

One reader per dimension, each **re-reading that dimension's actual material**.
The map says the house is 32%; it does not contain the story of the house.

**Cross-cutting practices do not get a folder of their own topic.** A habit
appearing identically in the house notes, the hobby notes and the work notes
belongs in a practices record. Filing it under any one domain recreates the
blindness that hid it.

**Definition of done: the house shows new records, not new indexes** — and every
one of them carries the fields in the next section, because a record that names
nobody and no date is not a catalogued record either.

### `epistemic_status` comes from a list, and this is the list

**Almost nothing tells a writing AI what the allowed values are.** So an importer
sees the field on records that already exist, copies the shape, and invents a value
that reads perfectly and ranks nothing. **The failure, measured.** One world's
mailbox import produced **fourteen invented statuses across 55 records** —
`primary-correspondence-mixed-speakers`, `source-grounded-synthesis`,
`operational-record`. Every one plausible. Every one outside the vocabulary, so
every one unrankable by anything deciding how much weight a record may carry.

**The whole list, strongest first, and what each one is for:**

| Value | When |
|---|---|
| `primary-artifact` | The thing itself — an email, a letter, a photo, a receipt |
| `primary-artifact-degraded` | The thing itself, only partly captured |
| `verbatim-computer-transcription` | A machine's transcript of audio |
| `faithful-transcription-of-primary-artifact` | A careful transcription of one |
| `published-fellowship-literature` | A published text, quoted from the text |
| `physiological_measured` | An instrument's reading |
| `directly-reported` | I said it, in my own voice |
| `sourced` | Attributed to something nameable |
| `derived-from-primary-artifact` | Read off a primary — a summary, a reconciliation |
| `derived-interpretation` | A reading somebody made |
| `inferred` | Worked out rather than stated |
| `speculative` | A guess, labelled |
| `unresolved` | Genuinely open |
| `existence-verified-not-read` | Held material: it exists, its words were never read |

**A qualifying parenthetical is fine and the base word is what counts** —
`sourced (self-report, 2019)` is `sourced`. **A compound is not.** If none of the
fourteen fits, take the nearest weaker one and add a note. Never a fifteenth word: an
invented status is not a finer distinction, it is a record that has stopped answering
the only question the field exists to answer.

### Write the graph down, or it does not exist

**This is the section most likely to be skimmed and it is the one that decides
whether any of the work above compounds.** Everything before this is about what to
*notice*. This is about what to *write into front matter* — and noticing without
writing leaves no trace a later pass can use.

**The failure, measured.** One world held **2,144 records with front matter. 1,993
of them — 92% — carried no person, no place, no event and no source**, which means
they could not be connected to anything, by any tool, ever. **1,785 came from a
single notes import, and 100% of those had no person and no real date.**

Here is the part that matters: **501 of those imported records — 28% — named a
person who already had a file in that same world, in their body text. 1,245 such
mentions, sitting in prose where nothing could read them.** The information was
not missing. It was never lifted into a field.

**That import was extracted by a frontier model following this document, and it
followed it well.** Rule 6 of pass 1 says people matter more than subject matter;
the layers table says extract the graph from everything. Both were obeyed — in the
*reports*. **Nothing anywhere told the extractor that the report is thrown away and
the front matter is what survives.** Now it does.

| On every record you write | |
|---|---|
| `related_people` | everyone named **who has a record in `people/`** — see below |
| `places` | anywhere the thing happened or is about |
| `related_events` | events already in the world that this belongs to |
| `source_refs` | what this came from — a file, an upload, a conversation |
| `date` or `occurred_at` | **when it HAPPENED** |

**`created_at` is not a date.** It is when the *record* was written, and in a house
hydrated by sweeps that is simply the day an agent ran. In the archive above, **92%
of records carried an ingestion timestamp and nothing else**, so the first graph
built over them reported "same week" on nearly every pair: it was measuring the
import, not the life. **If the only date you can write is today's, the record has
no date — and it should say so rather than borrow one.**

**A rough date beats no date, every time.** `~2000`, `early 2000s`, `2002-fall` are
all fine and all useful; mark the softness with `date_status`. **A year is enough to
place a record in a life. Nothing is not.** Never round a guess up into a precise
date to make the field look tidy — precision you invented is worse than the vagueness
you actually have.

**One field name, never a synonym.** In that same world `source_ref` sits on 61
records and `source_refs` on 151, and **not one record carries both** — so a tool
reading the plural silently sees two thirds of the sources and reports a number that
looks fine. Whichever spelling the world already uses is the spelling. Check an
existing record before you invent a field, and if the corpus is already split, say so
rather than picking a side silently.

**The same rule applies to VALUES, and it breaks there more often.** A field with a
free vocabulary — tags, groupings, categories a world invents as it goes — grows
synonyms one day at a time, because each value gets written by a different pass with
a different source in front of it. In the same world a grouping field ended up with
`work` and `professional` side by side, holding the same kind of person: one read off
a folder name, the other off an exported contact list, and nothing read both. **Before
writing a new value into an open vocabulary, list the values already in use.** It costs
one query and it is the only thing standing between an open vocabulary and a pile of
near-duplicates that each look small enough to ignore.

**Check what a field MEANS before merging it into another one.** In the same audit
`location` looked like an obvious synonym for `places` — until someone read the
values, and 31 of its 34 were `in-repo`, `external` or `mixed`. It records where the
source material sits, not where anything happened. **A name collision is not a
synonym, and merging those two would have destroyed one field and polluted the
other.** Two fields are the same field when their VALUES are interchangeable; nothing
about the names can tell you that.

**One spelling per person, matching their record.** The graph joins on the string, so
a person written three ways is three people, each too rare to surface. Look them up;
do not go by ear.

**Unknown is a value — absent is not.** An empty field cannot be told apart from a
field nobody looked at, so the next pass has to redo the work to find out which. **If
you read the item and there genuinely was no person in it, write the empty list.**
That is a finding. Silence is not.

**Never invent an entity to fill a field.** An unnamed "my boss" or "a guy from the
gym" stays in the prose and out of `related_people`. A name you inferred is a name
that will be confirmed by nobody and quietly joined to by everything. **Empty beats
wrong here by a wide margin**, because these fields are trusted downstream in a way a
model's prose is not.

**AND A NAMED STRANGER IS THE SAME CASE.** `related_people` is a LINK field, not a
cast list: it points at records in `people/`, and a name in it with nothing behind it
points nowhere. The roster builder already drops such a name on the floor, so all it
does is fail my checks.

**Who earns a record is my life, not the document's word count.** Somebody who
matters gets a file and a link from everything they appear in. A name on an email, a
shop, a recruiter, a person quoted once, somebody's colleague — named in the writing,
absent from the front matter. A record for everyone ever mentioned turns that folder
into a contact list I did not ask for, and it means my private account of a stranger
outlives the sentence it was in.

**This is the failure mode of a mailbox import specifically.** A year of email names
hundreds of people and almost none of them are in my life; an extractor that
treats "appears in the corpus" as "belongs in the roster" produces a directory of
counterparties wearing the shape of a personal record.

**And never list me.** It is my house. I am not a related person in my own record.

**Do this while the item is open.** It is nearly free at extraction time and it is a
second full pass over the whole archive at any point after.

### Never derive structure from the part of a world that is already organised

**A reader that can only see the tidy folders will rediscover the tidiness and call
it a finding.** This is the priming failure again, wearing the costume of a data
source instead of a briefing, and it is harder to spot because nobody wrote a
biography — the bias arrives through which files were readable.

Measured: a tool set out to group a person's contacts into the circles of their life,
reading which top-level folder each person appeared in most. It ran over folders with
interpretable names — the equivalent of `recovery/`, `work/`, `cycling/` — **117 files
out of 2,148, or 5.4% of the world.** The 1,815-file folder holding the raw imported
archive, 85% of everything and the place the people actually are, was skipped because
its name did not describe a category.

Two things were wrong at once, and the second is the one to remember:

1. **The sample was 5%,** and not a random 5%.
2. **Those folders exist BECAUSE somebody already knew those were the topics.** An
   agent made them, earlier, from the same material. So the groupings could only ever
   return the organisation that was already there, restated with more confidence and a
   person's name attached.

The subject's verdict on the output was that everything but one bounded, long-finished
group was *"a massive fail"* — and the group that survived was the only one that had
not been inferred at all. It came from a list they had written themselves.

**The test, before deriving any structure from a corpus's shape:** what fraction of
the corpus can this see, and *who arranged the part it can see?* If the answer is
"a previous pass arranged it," the output is a mirror. **Structure has to come from the
unorganised bulk or it is not a finding** — which usually means reading content, and
that is the expensive thing the shortcut was trying to avoid.

### A correction is not another telling

**This section governs any submission, not only a big import.** Most of this
document is about material arriving for the first time. This is about material
arriving *against* something already on file, and it is the one case where
filing faithfully produces a worse record than not filing at all.

**How to recognise one.** The telling points at an existing record, Afterword or
claim and says it is wrong: *"that wasn't Tuesday, it was the Thursday"*,
*"you have my brother down as the one who called — it was my sister"*, *"this
corrects the Afterword about the parking lot."* The tell is a **reference plus a
contradiction**. Somebody re-telling the same afternoon with new detail is not
correcting anything; somebody saying a named thing on file is wrong is.

**The failure if you miss it.** Filed as an ordinary telling, the correction
becomes a second account sitting beside the first, and nothing anywhere says
which one is right. The wrong version is *older*, so every date-ordered view
puts it first and every later pass reads it as the established fact with a
variant attached. **The record now contains the error, the fix, and no way to
tell them apart** — which is worse than the error alone, because the error alone
was at least correctable.

**What to do instead — and it is not to edit the original.**

1. **File what they said as its own source**, verbatim, exactly as any telling.
2. **Name what it corrects** in its front matter: `corrects: <path or record_id>`.
   One field, that spelling, on the correction.
3. **Mark the original** with `corrected_by: <the new source>` and, where the
   original is a record rather than a source, fix the claim itself in place and
   say in the body that it was corrected, when, and on whose telling.
4. **Never delete, never silently overwrite, never sand down a locked file.**
   An Afterword in particular cannot be edited at all — correct the *records it was
   drawn from* and leave the Afterword as written.

**Why both survive.** The original misunderstanding and the later correction are
both true things about this house: one is what was believed, the other is what
turned out to be so. A record that can be quietly amended cannot be trusted
afterwards to say what was believed at the time — which is most of why anybody
would keep one.

**If it names a problem without naming the fix, ask.** *"You got that wrong"*
and *"it was actually my sister"* are different amounts of information, and only
the second one can be filed. Take down what is actually true before writing
anything; a correction that only records dissatisfaction corrects nothing.

### Whose voice is this?

**Before attributing any line to me, work out who was speaking.**

A personal archive is full of recorded speech: what a colleague said, what a
customer said, shorthand taken while someone talked, a line from a book, a
stranger's review pasted in during an argument. **All of it is in my file, in my
handwriting, and none of it is me.**

In the measured run **four records misattributed other people's words to the
owner in a single day** — a coaching line, a "when I have a bad day, people die"
line that came from a colleague's wife at a different company a decade earlier, a
financial-fear line that was shorthand from a direct report, and a remembered
date that turned out to belong to a stranger's complaint about the same
contractor. **Every one read as intimate self-disclosure. None of it was his.**

**A quote is not a finding. It is evidence to be clarified.** He clarified all
four in about a minute when asked. **Ask. If the note cannot say, mark it
unsourced rather than handing it to me.**

### Three layers, and they are not equally valuable

When I say a whole category is low-signal — "skip the work stuff, it says nothing
about me as a person" — **that is about content, not people.**

| Layer | Rule | Worth |
|---|---|---|
| **Content** — deals, logistics, specs | extract selectively | low |
| **Graph** — who, when, how often, how long | **extract from everything** | high |
| **Dynamics and interiority** — how people are with each other, and what I noticed | **hunt for it everywhere** | **the point** |

Dropping a "low-signal" work archive in the measured run also dropped **the work
half of the subject's social graph** — including three colleagues they named as
significant within the hour. And when that archive was finally read, its largest
component was **not deals. It was people** — 1:1s, coaching, hiring — by a wide
margin.

**Why the third layer is the product.** A recognition worth having is the moment
the world says something about someone that only their whole life could explain.
**Events, dates and achievements cannot deliver that** — they are the most-told,
least-revealing facts about anyone, the contents of a résumé and an obituary.
What delivers it is interior: how someone reacts under pressure, what they notice
about others, what they say when nobody is grading them.

In practice: in a 1:1 log, **skip the number.** Keep *"he shut down the moment I
pushed"* and *"I was managing his reaction instead of telling him the truth."*
The pipeline is content. **That second sentence is who someone is.**

### Who matters — two axes, and their failure modes

- **Density** (entries per month) finds the intense and bounded.
- **Durability** (total span) finds the low-and-steady.

**Rank on both**, and count **entries, not files** — one 1:1 log is a single file
holding forty conversations.

**Both axes fail in known ways. Guard them:**

**Durability is inflated by running notes.** For a list or log kept over years,
the creation date is when the *note* began, not when any line inside it was
written. In the measured run an eleven-year relationship turned out to be a
single vacation tip inside a running list created in 2012 and last edited in
2024; every other mention fell inside three months. **Before trusting a long
span, require corroboration in the early window and check the modified date
against the created date.**

**Frequency measures attention, not intimacy.** A person studied carefully
*because they hold power* ranks identically to a person who is loved. In the
measured run a boss's boss ranked sixth by mention count and was written up as a
close relationship; the subject's own verdict was that they barely knew the
person and simply knew how to play the game. **No count separates these. Only the person can.**

### What the method cannot do, and why to say so

**Artifact hydration recovers the people someone wrote about — not the people who
mattered.** Asked to name colleagues who mattered, the subject in the measured
run volunteered two names appearing in a 1,775-item archive **once and twice.**
Both ranking axes were correct by their own logic and wrong about that life.

**The only instrument that found them was the person saying their names out loud.** So
the job is not only to read what someone uploads. It is to notice the shape of
who and what is *missing* — and ask.

His framing, which is the right posture for all of this:

> this exercise isn't to define all truths about a person. It's to fill in some of
> the structural gaps in our understanding

### Scaling down

**Under ~100 items:** one agent, all four passes in series, same rules, same
blindness in pass 1. **The passes are about ordering, not headcount.**
**Over ~100:** fan out passes 1 and 3. **Pass 2 is always a single reader** —
the sum is the point, and it cannot be delegated to the readers being summed.

### Reading one source into records — the per-source pass

**This governs any single unread source**: a recording, somebody else's telling
that arrived as a contribution, a story I typed or pasted straight into a chat
and you filed. The method above is for piles; this is the pass an archive prompt
runs on each source, and the order is not negotiable.

1. **The source is already archived.** Verify it; do not re-file it.
2. **If the front matter says `subject_is_third_party: true`, stop here and make the
   artifact.** That source is an account of somebody who does not live in this house,
   given by somebody who knows them — a portrait, gathered so that person could be made
   something. Make what its `wanted:` field names, in the folder its `wanted_path:`
   names, and then stop: file NOTHING into my records, add nobody to `people/`, and
   write no Afterword. **That artifact IS the job.** Records and an Afterword are the
   wrong end state — they leave the person who asked for a letter needing a further step
   to get one, and they write a stranger, and everyone that stranger mentioned, into a
   roster of my life. The file's own opening paragraph says who it is for; read it.
3. **Otherwise establish whose story it is — from the transcript, never from the
   `subject:` field — then write the derived records.** A telling invited from
   someone else arrives in three shapes, and only the first is about me: their side
   of a memory I was in; their account of somebody I love, from before me or from
   rooms I was never in; or their own life, brushing mine only at its edges. This has
   already gone wrong in a real world: a contributor's telling from their own
   life — of meeting somebody its owner loves, years before its owner knew either
   of them — was filed as an event in its owner's chronology, and seeded the
   roster with people from the contributor's life.
   So: **if I do not appear in the telling, nothing from it enters my timeline**;
   any event or person record it produces is marked `subject_is_third_party: true`
   and says whose life it belongs to; and a "you" in the invitation's own topic or
   prompt addresses the contributor, never me. Then: people, places, events, turning
   points, time. Give a record in `people/` to everyone who MATTERS in my life, and
   link it from everything they appear in
   — **unless they exist only inside someone else's memory**, in which case they are
   named in that record and given no file in my roster. Everybody else is named in
   the writing and left out of the front matter: the link-field rule above says why,
   and a mailbox import is where it bites.
4. **Read back BOTH directions.** Write on the SOURCE which records came out of
   it, and on each RECORD which source it came from. This is the step that gets
   skipped, and skipping it is the whole failure: a transcript on disk, records
   citing it, and nothing on the source saying it was ever read — so the next
   agent re-reads the same source and writes everything twice.
5. **Write me the Afterword** — where this source earns one. The test is the
   three sections: what happened, what sits underneath it in my own record, and
   what you make of it. **A source that cannot fill all three is not an
   Afterword** — file its records, say in one line that there was nothing to
   read back, and go on to the next. Do not manufacture the middle section to
   clear the gate; an invented connection is worse than no Afterword. The one
   narrow exception is a world too young to reach into at all, where the first
   few tellings are written without it.
   Otherwise one per source, never one per batch — with two exceptions. Sources
   batch-uploaded from a single event — several recordings
   from one ride, one trip, one afternoon — are one telling arriving in parts,
   and get **one Afterword covering the batch**, anchored to the fullest source
   and citing every part in its `source_refs`. And a later batch from the same
   event **revises that Afterword to a new version** — a new `-v2` file, the
   same `record_id`, `version: 2` in the front matter, and every version kept
   in the folder — never a sibling, and never a deletion.
   **The second exception is the pile this whole contract is about: an import is
   ONE act.** A mailbox export, a notes archive, a photo library becomes hundreds
   of sources because that is how it was cut up, not because hundreds of things
   were said — so it gets ONE Afterword about the import, anchored to the batch
   receipt or the fullest source, never one per item. An item inside it earns its
   own only where it would have earned one arriving alone, which is a handful out
   of hundreds. A single mailbox import once wrote forty-six Afterwords in an
   afternoon, most of them two sections about an errand, and the shelf holding a
   life's read-backs became a mail log.
   [afterwords/README.md](afterwords/README.md) is the binding contract; read it
   before the first one. **Step 4 and step 5 are different things and the words
   are confusingly close.** Step 4's "read back" is bookkeeping — arrows between
   a source and its records, so nothing gets read twice. Step 5 is a piece of
   writing addressed to me, the reason saying something to this world is worth
   doing at all. Doing the bookkeeping and stopping leaves me a tidier
   repository and nothing to show for the telling.
6. **End the reply with each Afterword's link** —

## Two optional lists on any record you write

Any substantive record — a person, an event, a story, a reflection, a synthesis,
a practice, a venture — may carry two lists in its front matter:

```yaml
questions:
  - What happened when you got back to the house?
noticings:
  - This is the third time you have described leaving before the end.
```

They are how the record offers something back when I open it. **Both are
optional, both are frequently empty, and `[]` is a finished answer rather than
a gap to fill.** Write them when you have just read enough to have earned one —
never later, and never by opening a record to think of some.

**`questions`** — up to three things I could say more about. Each has to be
worth pressing record for.

- **The test that kills most of them: could you ask this of anybody?** If yes,
  cut it. *"What made that special?"* flatters, can be answered without
  thinking, and tells me you were not really listening.
- **Ask for a telling, not an answer.** Nothing I can dispatch in four words.
- **Do not lead.** A question may not walk me toward what you already concluded.
- **Never ask about somebody else's interior.** "Why did she do that" invites me
  to invent another person's motives as fact. Ask what I saw, said, or did.
- **How many is not a target.** If one question is worth asking, write one.

### A question may come from what you know, not only from what is missing

If something you know about the world would explain a gap, **ask it — do not
assert it.** *"I see the school you left had no team for that sport. Did that
play a part in the move?"* A wrong assertion is a defect in my record; a wrong
question costs nothing, because I just say no. And what I confirm becomes
something I said, which is better evidence than anything you could have reasoned
your way to.

Three rules on those:

- **Say how sure you are, and mean it.** The hedge is not manners, it is you
  reporting your own confidence, and it is the difference between a good question
  and an irritating one. Being wrong is fine. Being wrong while sounding certain
  is what costs you my attention.

  - near-certain and checkable — just ask: *"That club was a summer program,
    right?"*
  - plausible and specific — name it as yours: *"I see the school you left had no
    team for that sport. Did that have anything to do with the move?"*
  - a real reach — say so FIRST: *"This may be completely unrelated, so take it
    with a grain of salt, but…"*

  **Grade it honestly.** If every question opens with "this might be nothing" the
  hedge stops meaning anything and becomes a tic, which is worse than not hedging
  at all — I can no longer tell when you actually are unsure.

  And **hedge before, not after.** "This might be nothing, but I see X" and "I see
  X — this might be nothing" are not the same sentence. The second makes me take
  X as a fact before the disclaimer arrives, so I have to back out of something I
  had already started believing.

- **Carry your premise.** Say the thing you noticed and mark it as yours, then
  ask — *"I see X. Did that matter?"*, never *"Given X, why did you…"*. If X is
  wrong I have to be able to correct X, not just answer around it.
- **A fact or a cause, never a motive.** *"That program was ranked first in the
  state — was that a factor?"* is a question. *"Were you trying to prove
  something to your father?"* is an interpretation with a question mark on it,
  and it is the same trespass as asking about somebody else's interior, turned
  on me.

- **No anchor, no question.** A connection has to hang off something I actually
  said — a time, a place, a thing I named. Confidence does not substitute for
  this: a well-hedged question about something I never mentioned is still you
  guessing at my life, and no amount of "this might be nothing" makes it land.

**`noticings`** — observations, not asks. No question mark. What earns the slot
is **two things in view at once**: a friction between records, something I said
years ago against something I said last week. A noticing that only restates the
record it sits on is not one.

- **State the observation, never the interpretation.** *"Third time you have
  described leaving before the end"* — not *"that must still be hard."*
- **A true fact is not automatically worth saying.** How long a file is, when it
  was uploaded, how many records mention somebody — those are facts about the
  apparatus, not about me.
- **Say nothing rather than something small.** Silence costs nothing; a weak
  noticing costs the slot and teaches me to stop reading that part of the page.

**Nothing that belongs in these lists may appear in the prose.** A question
written into the body is invisible to the control that is built from the field,
and then appears twice for anyone reading the record on the web.

**Where they do NOT go.** Not on a README, an index, or anything under
`sources/` — a source is evidence, and the controls are for what was made of
it. Not on product docs, decision records or anything describing how the
software works.
