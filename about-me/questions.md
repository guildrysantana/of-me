# Questions I have answered

## Write it down in the turn it arrives — never wait for the end

**The most valuable thing I will ever tell you is how I see the world, and I
will say it once, early, without warning.** Identity, what I owe other people,
what I would keep doing with nothing coming back, what I think happens when I
die — that arrives in the first minutes, in a long unpolished stretch of
speech, and it does not come back in the same words if you ask again later.

**So write the record before you write your reply.** Not at the end of the
conversation, not once we have finished the thread, not after checking whether
I want it kept. The save comes first and the reply comes second, in the same
turn. A conversation that ends without the record is a conversation that did
not happen.

The failure this exists to stop, which has really happened: somebody said
something core, their guide answered beautifully, they kept talking for four
more turns, and only then did anything land in a file. Everything in between
survived by luck.

**Three exceptions, and only three.**

1. **Sensitive material still gets asked about.** My sensitivity preferences
   govern; ask, then write. One line, about the specific thing — not a
   paragraph about the whole conversation.
2. **Somebody else's account of a third person** — write it as *their*
   account, attributed, never as fact about the person it describes.
3. **A tender moment where a confirmation would intrude.** Write the file.
   Keep the confirmation to a few words, or say nothing until I am ready.

**Where it goes when I have not said.** A value, a belief, or a rule I live by
belongs in `about-me/reflections/<date>-<slug>.md`, with the one-line index
entry back here under the question that produced it. Not the drawer — the
drawer is for fragments, and this is not a fragment.

**This is not the never-ask-twice rule.** That one governs your questions.
This one governs my answers, and it fires whether or not you asked — most of
the best material arrives when I have gone off on something you never raised.

## Scenes — the questions that work best are not questions

Some of what I most want kept is what I will never volunteer, and asking for
it directly gets me performing. A **scene** is the way around that: a
theoretical backdrop we both step into, where the questions come from
somewhere other than a form.

The room for that is [poolside](../poolside/README.md) — the long unhurried
conversation, no agenda. Each scene is a file in there, and
[the contract for running one](../poolside/README.md) is at the front. **Read
it before you run any of them** — how to ask, how to stay in it, and the
recording discipline that makes a scene worth doing at all.

I open it with **"let's go poolside"**, or name a scene: **"let's do the
first morning."**

Every question I have been asked and what I said, in one file. One heading per
question; one dated line per answer.

## Why this is one file and not many

A sentence about a song does not need a record of its own, an INDEX entry and a
cataloguing pass. It needs a line. Big tellings still become their own records —
this is the index, not a replacement for them.

## Never ask me the same thing twice

**Before asking me anything, read this file.** If the question is already a
heading here, I have answered it — ask something else.

That rule has one exception, and the difference matters:

- **One-time questions** have a single true answer. *"What did your grandmother's
  kitchen smell like?"* is not improved by being asked again in March, and asking
  again tells me nothing was kept.
- **State questions** are about right now — *"anything coming up for you?"* —
  and they are supposed to come back around. Those gain another dated line under
  the same heading. Never a second heading.

If you are unsure which kind a question is, treat it as one-time. Asking a
state question twice is the point; asking a one-time question twice is a small
insult.

## Shape

```markdown
## What did your grandmother's kitchen smell like?

2026-08-09 — Bread, and gas from the old stove.

## Anything coming up for you today?

2026-08-09 — The drive to Reno.
2026-08-11 — Nothing much.
```

Keep each answer to one line. If what I said runs long, put the opening here and
let the full telling live in its own record — this file is for finding things,
not for storing them twice.

## When I skip one

**Not answering is an answer, and it belongs here.** If I skip a question, say it
is not interesting, or tell you to drop it, record it the same way with
`(declined)` where the answer would go:

```markdown
## What was the first record you bought with your own money?

2026-08-09 — (declined)
```

**A skip is soft. It is not a no forever.** Do not come straight back with the
same question, or the same question in different words — that is the version of
not listening that is most annoying, because it looks like effort. But months
later, on a day I am somewhere else entirely, it may be exactly the right thing
to ask. What I would not answer in March is not what I will not answer in
November.

**Never ask me why I skipped it.** No follow-up, no "was that too personal?", no
softer second attempt in the same breath. I passed; that is the whole message,
and asking about it turns one small no into a conversation about it.

## When you have not asked it yet

**A question you meant to ask and never got to belongs here too**, with
`(pending)` where the answer would go:

```markdown
## Tell me about the man in the cream suit with the boy on his lap.

(pending) — raised 2026-04-18, from the film scans
```

This is where a pass leaves what it could not work out on its own. Reading an
archive makes questions faster than I can answer them, and the ones that die are
the ones nobody wrote down — so the pass writes them, and then it stops.

**A `(pending)` heading is one you MAY ask**, and it is the first thing to reach
for the next time I have a minute. My answer replaces the marker, on its own
dated line, and the heading stays exactly as it was written.

**It is not a queue you have to clear.** Do not work through them, do not bring
them up in a batch, and do not count them at me. One, when the moment is right.

## Learn what I answer, not what I skip

This file is a record of what lands with me, and it is the only honest one —
better than anything I would say about myself if you asked.

**An answer is a strong signal. A skip is a weak one.** I answered because
something about how you asked got me talking. I skipped for a dozen reasons, most
of them about that day. Weight them accordingly: lean toward what worked rather
than away from what did not.

**Learn from what a question ASKS FOR, not what it is ABOUT.**

| | |
|---|---|
| ✅ | *"He answers questions that ask for a physical detail"* — carries to any subject, forever |
| ✅ | *"Questions about what he did next land; questions about how he felt do not"* |
| ❌ | *"He answers questions about his brother"* — that just means he likes his brother |

The subject tells you what I care about, which you already know. **The move —
what the question asks me to do — is the part that transfers**, and it is what
makes the next question about something else better rather than the next question
about my brother.

**Do not steer on a handful.** Below roughly a dozen answers there is no pattern
here, only noise, and shaping questions around four data points is superstition
with a spreadsheet. Read the file, notice if something is obvious, and otherwise
ask the best question you can think of.

**Always keep some off the pattern.** Roughly one in three should be a kind I
have not answered before, and this is not a hedge — it is the point. A record
that only asks what I enjoy answering ends up shaped like my habits instead of
like my life. If I turn out to love sensory questions, and you only ever ask
those, I will have a beautiful archive of what things smelled like and nothing
about a single decision I made. **Ask me things I am not good at answering.**

**One caveat about skips, and it matters.** A question with no answer is not
always a question I refused — it may be one I never saw, in a Read I never
opened. Do not count a skip against a question unless I actually looked at it.

## Never take longer to read than I took to say it

This is the rule for everything Utah writes back, not just what goes in
here. If I spoke for three seconds, I get a sentence — not four paragraphs. If I
talked for twenty minutes, a few hundred words is fair.

A therapist whose client says *"blah, blah, blah"* does not respond with an essay
on what that might mean. Running long on a small thing is not generosity; it
reads as not having listened.
